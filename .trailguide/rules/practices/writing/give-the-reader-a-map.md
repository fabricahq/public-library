## Give the reader a map, then follow it

Structure a long piece so the reader always knows where they are:

1. **Opening:** Establish the problem, question, or experience and why it matters to the intended reader. A concrete observation can do this without a dramatic claim.
2. **Map:** For a piece with several independent points, preview them before the detailed argument, in a sentence or list. For an investigation or story, establish the question and let each section lead to the next discovery.
3. **Headings:** Name what each section contributes. Use claims, questions, or specific topic labels, whichever makes the route easier to follow. Deliver any promised points in order.
4. **Close:** Resolve the opening question or explain what follows from the argument. A recommendation, a remaining uncertainty, or a next step can provide closure without repeating every section.

### Exceptions

- A short announcement or single-point note may need neither a roadmap nor headings. State what changed and why it matters.
- Choose structure for the reader's task, not a fixed word count, number of points, or screen height.
- Familiar labels such as "Installation" or "Conclusion" are fine when they help readers find what they need. A label is weak when it could describe almost any section.

### Rationale

Readers skim before they commit. An opening establishes a reason to read; a roadmap or sequence of discoveries helps them stay oriented. Brikman's essays use both explicit outlines and unfolding investigations. The useful habit is guiding the reader through the argument, not imposing the same template on every piece.

### Examples

#### Application: opening a post

**Incorrect (counterexample):**

> Over the past several months, we have been working on a number of tools and have learned many things along the way that we'd like to share in this post.

This spends the reader's first sentence on throat-clearing. It doesn't say what the reader will get.

**Correct:**

> An agent can finish a patch before you've finished reviewing its last one. How do you keep up? This post covers:
>
> - Which review mistakes keep recurring
> - Where skills help and where they fall short
> - How rules can address the remaining gaps

This illustrative opening names a reader's problem and promises a route through it. The body must deliver the promised comparison.

#### Application: section headings

**Incorrect (counterexample):**

```markdown
## Background
## Considerations
## Conclusion
```

As the only headings in a comparison of skills and rules, these hide what each section contributes.

**Correct:**

```markdown
## Skills are coarse-grained
## Updating skills is awkward
## Rules let you select and update guidance separately
```

These headings expose the comparison. Specific topic labels can work too: Brikman's essay on vibe coding uses "Maturity" and "Maintenance" to match its opening roadmap.

#### Application: a valid short post

A 300-word note announcing a bug fix opens with what changed and why it matters. It needs no map or headings, so it needs no change.

### Validation

- Read the opening and state the question, problem, or benefit it gives the reader.
- If there is a roadmap, check that the body delivers its points in order. Otherwise, check that each section advances the opening question.
- Read the headings alone. Check that they distinguish the sections and make relevant material easy to find.
- Check that the ending resolves the opening promise or identifies what remains unresolved. A recap is optional.
